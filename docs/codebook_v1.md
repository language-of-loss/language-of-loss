# Gold-set codebook v1 (clause level), 2026-10-10

Read this before you code `data/annotation/gold_sheet_v1_coder{1,2}.csv` or the calibration pilot
`gold_pilot_r{1,2}.csv`. Status: **draft v1 for the pilot**. Definitions come from Methodology v3 (clause coding) and
the lexicon v0.2 conventions (orientation R = ritual; process L/Rs; function Sk/Of; grammar_forced Y/N/context). They
have not yet been tested for agreement; the pilot is meant to find where they fail.

Example clauses are cited by `clause_id` only. Their text is in **`docs/codebook_v1_examples.md`**, which is local
and git-ignored (third-party text never goes into git). The tables below are generated by
`src/analysis/codebook_examples.py`; edit the examples there, not here. All examples come from dev-split texts, and
none of them appears in your sheets.

## How to code

- **Unit.** One row is one finite clause (`clause_text`). `span_text` is the same clause with its punctuation and
  connectives; `context_before` / `context_after` are the neighbouring clauses. Use the context to resolve dropped
  subjects, pronouns and who is dead, but code **only the clause in `clause_text`**.
- **One value per field**, spelled exactly as in the tables (the agreement script ignores case but flags anything
  outside the list). If two values fit, take the one the decision rule gives and write the other in `notes`.
- **Who died.** You do not have the source text. If the context does not tell you whether a person is the deceased,
  code your best reading and write `who died?` in `notes`.
- **Do not look up machine labels.** The keys and every machine-label file are kept away from coders on purpose.
- Language is never evidence of religion, nationality or culture: code what the clause says.

## 1. orientation (I / F / C / R / NA)

**Definition.** Whose grief world the clause places the loss in. `I` (individual): the loss, grief or bond is framed as
the writer's or one person's own inner experience. `F` (familial): it is framed through the family as a unit: kin
bonds, the household, family roles and duties. `C` (communal): it is framed through a wider group: village, society,
mourners, friends, "everyone", shared norms. `R` (ritual): the clause uses or reports a conventional mourning formula
or rite (श्रद्धांजलि, पुण्यतिथि, अंतिम संस्कार, RIP, funeral, cremation, kriya). `NA`: the clause has no grief, loss,
mourning or bond content (background narrative, logistics, the deceased's life told without reference to the loss).

**Decision rule.** (1) Is there grief / loss / mourning / bond content? If not, `NA`. (2) Is a formula or rite named or
performed? Then `R`. (3) Otherwise ask who carries the loss in the clause: one person → `I`; the family as such → `F`;
a group beyond the family → `C`.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *Religious content is never R by itself.* God, prayer, मोक्ष, Jannah or faith go to `target = God` (and, for the
   analysts, the lexicon's `cultural_script` tag). Orientation stays what the rest of the clause says (`I`, `F`, `C`);
   `R` needs a mourning formula or rite.
2. *A kin word does not make a clause F.* "I lost my father" / मैंने पिता को खोया is `I`: the loss is the writer's;
   the kin relation shows up in `target`. Code `F` only when the family as a unit or its roles carry the clause (हम
   सबका सहारा चला गया, "our family will never be the same").
3. *F vs C with हम / we.* हम / we meaning the household is `F`; हम / we meaning mourners, the village, society or
   "people like us" is `C`. If you cannot tell, code `F` and note `हम scope?`.

## 2. experiencer (self / kin / community / deceased / God / impersonal / other)

**Definition.** The class of the being who experiences, undergoes or does what the predicate says, i.e. the logical
subject. In Hindi this is often not the nominative: in मुझे दुख हुआ the experiencer is मुझे (`self`). `self` = the
writer; `kin` = a living relative; `community` = a group (हम/we as a group, family as a whole, people, society,
friends, mourners); `deceased` = the person who died (by name, kin term, pronoun or `you` addressed to them); `God`;
`impersonal` = no sentient experiencer (things, events, abstractions, it/this, weather, time); `other` = a non-kin
individual (doctor, friend, employer, the reader as `you`).

**Decision rule.** Find the predicate; ask who feels / undergoes / does it; recover dropped subjects from context.
Class by identity, not by grammar: a kin term naming the deceased is `deceased`, not `kin`.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *हम for a singular self.* Hindi writers often use हम / हमें for "I". If the context shows one person (singular
   events, मैं elsewhere in the same scene), code `self` and note `हम=sg`; otherwise `community`.
2. *Dative and body-part experiencers.* मुझे दुख हुआ, उन्हें गुस्सा आया, "it pains me", "my heart broke",
   आँखें भर आईं: the experiencer is the person (`self`, `deceased`, …), never `impersonal`. `impersonal` is only for
   clauses with no sentient experiencer at all.
3. *Deceased vs kin vs other for pronouns and `you`.* वे / she / he is `deceased` only if the context shows the
   referent died; a living parent is `kin`. `you` addressed to the dead is `deceased`; `you` addressed to readers is
   `other`; generic `you` ("people always tell you") is `community`.

## 3. predicate_type (state / action / event_befalling / speech / other)

**Definition.** What kind of predicate the clause asserts. `state`: being, having, feeling as a condition (was, is
sad, दुखी है, had no equal, feel guilty). `action`: a deliberate doing (took a flight, ले गए, made, cooked).
`event_befalling`: something happens to someone, outside their control: dying, illness, a feeling that comes over
the experiencer (मुझे दुख हुआ, याद आ गई, it hit me, it pains me, passed away, स्वर्गवास हो गया). `speech`: saying,
telling, asking, writing to, praying (as an act of speaking), calling. `other`: no state, action, event or speech is
asserted: wishes, blessings, thanks, greetings and imperatives as formulas (खुश रहो, सुकून दे, thank you, rest in
peace).

**Decision rule.** Look at the main predicate of `clause_text` (the finite verb or copula + complement): formula/wish →
`other`; verb of saying → `speech`; non-volitional happening → `event_befalling`; condition → `state`; else `action`.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *Death verbs are events, not actions.* died, passed away, चल बसे, गुज़र गए, नहीं रहे → `event_befalling`, even
   with a nominative subject. चले गए "went away" for a death is `event_befalling`; चले गए for a real trip is `action`.
2. *State vs event for feelings.* A feeling held as a condition (I am sad, मैं दुखी हूँ, I feel guilty) is `state`; a
   feeling that comes / happens / strikes (मुझे दुख हुआ, रोना आ गया, grief hit me) is `event_befalling`.
3. *Speech vs other.* "I said to her", उन्होंने कहा → `speech` (the quoted words are their own clauses). Formulaic
   thanks, blessings and wishes, even when addressed to someone, are `other`.

## 4. target (self / kin / community / deceased / God / impersonal / other / none)

**Definition.** The class of the person or thing the predicate is directed at: object, recipient, addressee, the
stimulus of a feeling, the goal of a prayer, the one whose loss is meant. Same classes as `experiencer`, plus `none`
when the predicate has no such participant (intransitive, copular, weather, dates).

**Decision rule.** Ask "to / at / about whom or what?" Take the first human or divine participant if there is one
(object, recipient, addressee, possessor of the lost thing), otherwise the main non-human object (`impersonal`),
otherwise `none`. A vocative addressed to the deceased counts as the target.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *"I lost my father".* experiencer `self`, target `deceased`: an individual subject with a relational target. Do
   not move the experiencer to `kin` or `community` because the object is a relative; the relational reading lives in
   `target`.
2. *Vocative to the dead.* Invented examples: माँ, तुम कहाँ चली गईं? / "Dad, I still talk to you": target
   `deceased`, even if the addressee is not a grammatical object.
3. *Religious content.* Prayer to, faith in, a gift from God / ईश्वर / Allah → target `God`; this is where religious
   content is coded, not in orientation. Predicate nominals that describe the subject (she was a teacher; वे गुजराती
   थे) are `none`, not a target.

## 5. process (L / Rs / NA)

**Definition.** The dual-process reading of the clause (lexicon v0.2: L = loss-oriented, Rs = restoration-oriented).
`L`: the clause dwells on the loss itself: the death, absence, pain, missing, the last days, what can no longer be.
`Rs`: the clause turns to living on: coping, new roles and duties, moving on, carrying the deceased's values forward,
advice about getting through. `NA`: neither (background with no grief content).

**Decision rule.** No grief content → `NA` (usually together with orientation `NA`). Otherwise: facing / feeling the
loss → `L`; adjusting to life after it → `Rs`.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *The death announcement* (passed away, निधन हो गया) is `L`, not `NA`: it states the loss.
2. *Memories.* Reminiscing about the deceased with a sense of absence is `L`; using the memory to carry something
   forward ("I will keep your ideals alive", आपके आदर्शों को जीवित रखूँगी) is `Rs`; a neutral episode with no loss
   content is `NA`.
3. *Advice to readers about coping* (find a therapist, time heals) is `Rs` (and function `Of`).

## 6. function (Sk / Of / NA)

**Definition.** Whether the clause seeks or offers support (lexicon v0.2: Sk = seek, Of = offer). `Sk`: the writer
asks for or expresses a need for comfort, prayers, help or presence (please pray, I needed her, let me grieve). `Of`:
the clause offers comfort, blessing, condolence, advice or prayer to someone (to the dead, the family, the reader), or
reports such an offer by others (people told me हौसला रख). `NA`: no support move.

**Decision rule.** Is support asked for (`Sk`) or given (`Of`) in this clause? If neither, `NA`. Most narrative clauses
are `NA`.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *Prayers for the deceased's peace* (आत्मा को शांति मिले, may Allah grant him Jannah, rest in peace) are `Of`
   (offered to the dead), not `Sk`.
2. *Reported vs own.* Comfort others gave the writer is `Of` (the offer is in the clause); the writer asking readers
   for prayers or comments is `Sk`.
3. *Kindness in the deceased's life* (he helped everyone, सबकी सेवा करते थे) is character description → `NA`, not
   `Of`.
English `Sk` is rare in the dev texts: the second English example is borderline and is there to be discussed in the
pilot.

## 7. grammar_forced (Y / N / context)

**Definition.** Whether the clause's experiencer framing or honorific form is imposed by the grammar. `Y`: the clause
has an experiencer predicate with no alternative framing of the same meaning (Hindi: याद आना, लगना, पता चलना,
INF + पड़ना / चाहिए, a named illness हो; English: miss, love, cry, glad, afraid, which have no it-/body-part twin), or
honorific plural agreement for a deceased parent, grandparent or elder. `context`: honorific plural for a deceased
spouse, sibling, child, friend or peer (forced only by the writer's relation to them). `N`: everything else, including
experiencer predicates that have a twin with the other framing, and clauses with no experiencer predicate or honorific
reference.

**Decision rule.** (1) Honorific plural for the deceased? elder → `Y`, other relation → `context`. (2) Experiencer
predicate with no twin? → `Y`. (3) Otherwise `N`. If (1) and (2) disagree, any `Y` wins.

**Commonest confusions**

   any site is forced; note the chosen one, since dative_alternative_available records it.

3. *No construction, no honorific*: `N`, never `context`. `context` is only for honorific plural with a non-elder.

## 8. dative_alternative_available (Y / N / NA)

**Definition.** For a clause with an experiencer predicate: was the other framing available with the same meaning?
In Hindi the two framings are dative (मुझे दुख है) and nominative (मैं दुखी हूँ). **In English the same field
records the English counterpart** (person subject ↔ it / body-part / stimulus subject: "I feel guilty" ↔ "guilt
weighs on me"; "it pains me" ↔ "I am pained"), following the eligibility detector's twin pairs. `Y`: a twin
construction exists, whichever framing the writer used. `N`: the predicate has no twin (the framing was forced). `NA`:
the clause has no experiencer predicate.

**Decision rule.** No experiencer predicate → `NA`. Otherwise ask whether the same meaning can be said with the other
framing *using the same root* (दुख होना ~ दुखी होना, रोना आना ~ रोना, guilt ~ guilty): yes → `Y`, no → `N`.

**Commonest confusions**
1. *Near-synonyms with a different root are not twins*: पता होना vs जानना, याद आना vs याद करना (involuntary vs
   deliberate), afraid vs "it frightened me" → `N`.
2. *Obligation and need* (INF + पड़ना, चाहिए, ज़रूरत होना) have no nominative twin → `N`, not `NA`: there is an
   experiencer predicate.
3. *`NA` vs `N`*: `NA` only when there is no experiencer predicate at all (actions, descriptions, dates). For English
   this field is an extension of the Hindi design and needs the investigators' confirmation before the main round.
